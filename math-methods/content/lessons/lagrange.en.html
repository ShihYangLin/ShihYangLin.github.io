EQUALITY-CONSTRAINED OPTIMIZATION · §§12.1–12.5, 12.7

Choosing along a feasible path

At a glance

  1. $Z=f+\lambda(c-g)$: $f_x=\lambda g_x$, $f_y=\lambda g_y$, $g(x,y)=c$

    Each choice's marginal gain per unit of constraint use is equal: $f_x/g_x=f_y/g_y=\lambda$. These conditions only find candidates. Why? ↓

  2. $|\bar H|>0\Rightarrow$ local max, $|\bar H|<0\Rightarrow$ local min

    With two variables and one equality, use the bordered Hessian: a zero corner and the constraint gradient on the border. Why? ↓

  3. $\lambda^*=\dfrac{df^*}{dc}$, $\Delta f^*\approx\lambda^*\Delta c$

    The multiplier is the shadow value of relaxing the constraint; for a finite change it is only an approximation. Why? ↓

  4. Keep $c-g$ in the Lagrangian; reversing it flips the multiplier's sign. Common mistakes ↓

Motivation

Independent choices cease to be independent when they share a budget, quota, or output requirement. A consumer with positive marginal utility would prefer more of each good, but a fixed budget limits the combinations that can be bought. A firm asked to deliver a specified output cannot choose labor and capital freely without meeting the order. The constraint cuts a path through the choice space. The economically relevant optimum is the highest or lowest objective value along that path, even if a better value exists at an infeasible point elsewhere on the surface.

One can sometimes substitute the constraint into the objective and solve a one-variable problem. The Lagrange method keeps both choices and the restriction visible in a single expression. We use one sign convention consistently: $Z=f+\lambda(c-g)$ for the equality $g=c$. This sign matters when interpreting the multiplier. We first derive stationary equations, then test curvature only in feasible directions, and finally interpret the multiplier in consumer and producer examples. All examples here have an interior positive solution; boundary and inequality problems belong to a different method.

Key ideas

$$Z(x,y,\lambda)=f(x,y)+\lambda[c-g(x,y)],\quad f_x=\lambda g_x,\quad f_y=\lambda g_y,\quad g(x,y)=c$$

The two marginal equations balance the objective gain from a choice against the constraint it uses. The third equation enforces feasibility. At a consumer optimum, $U_x/p_x=U_y/p_y=\lambda$: the marginal utility from the last unit of spending is equalized across goods. For a producer minimizing cost at a required output, $w/Q_L=r/Q_K=\lambda$: the spending needed for one more marginal unit of output is equalized across inputs. A tangency or stationary point alone is not enough to prove the desired maximum or minimum.

$$\bar H=\begin{pmatrix}0&g_x&g_y\\g_x&Z_{xx}&Z_{xy}\\g_y&Z_{yx}&Z_{yy}\end{pmatrix},\quad |\bar H|>0\Rightarrow\text{local max},\quad |\bar H|<0\Rightarrow\text{local min}$$

This site writes the bordered Hessian with the constraint gradient across the top and left and a zero in the corner; other arrangements can change the determinant's sign. A feasible small movement satisfies $g_xdx+g_ydy=0$. Only movements obeying that relation count: unrestricted Hessian curvature can include directions the decision-maker cannot take. The determinant sign is the opposite of the second-order quadratic form along the feasible direction. For two variables and one equality, a positive bordered determinant establishes a strict local maximum; a negative one establishes a strict local minimum. A zero determinant leaves the test inconclusive.

$$\lambda^*=\frac{d f^*(c)}{dc},\qquad \Delta f^*\approx\lambda^*\Delta c$$

With the declared convention, the multiplier is the shadow value of relaxing the right side of the equality. In utility maximization it measures marginal utility of extra budget. In cost minimization at a fixed output target it measures marginal cost of requiring another unit of output. The displayed change formula is a first-order approximation at the original constraint value. If the optimized value is curved as the constraint changes, a finite change requires re-solving and will generally differ from that linear estimate. A negative multiplier from the opposite Lagrangian sign cannot be inserted into this convention without adjustment.

Cobb–Douglas preferences provide a useful interior example: positive prices and income keep the budget line in the positive quadrant, and the utility level sets yield a unique tangency for positive exponents. For production, a positive Cobb–Douglas technology gives an isoquant for a required output; the least-cost isocost line is tangent where marginal products per unit of input price are balanced. Curvature along the feasible path and economically meaningful positive choices remain part of the conclusion. The method organizes these conditions; it does not replace checking them.

Worked examples

Quadratic objective on a line

Maximize $f=-(x-5)^2-(y-4)^2$ subject to $x+y=5$. With $Z=f+\lambda(5-x-y)$, the equations $-2(x-5)=\lambda=-2(y-4)$ and $x+y=5$ give $(x^*,y^*,\lambda^*)=(3,2,4)$. The border $(1,1)$ and Hessian diagonal $(-2,-2)$ yield $|\bar H|=4>0$, so this is a strict constrained maximum.

Budgeted utility

Maximize $U=xy$ with $2x+y=12$. Write $Z=xy+\lambda(12-2x-y)$. The FOCs give $y=2\lambda$, $x=\lambda$, and the budget then gives $(x^*,y^*,\lambda^*)=(3,6,3)$. The marginal utility per price is $6/2=3/1=3$. With $(g_x,g_y)=(2,1)$ and $U_{xy}=1$, the bordered determinant is $4>0$.

Least-cost inputs and shadow value

Minimize $C=4L+3K$ to produce $Q_0=12$ from $Q=LK$. Set $Z=4L+3K+\lambda(12-LK)$. The equations $4=\lambda K$, $3=\lambda L$, $LK=12$ give $(L^*,K^*,\lambda^*)=(3,4,1)$. Here $|\bar H|=-24<0$, so cost is locally minimized. Substituting $K=12/L$ gives $C=4L+36/L$, whose positive-domain minimum is at $L=3$. The multiplier says the optimized cost rises at one unit per marginal unit of required output.

Common mistakes

Keep $c-g$ in the stated Lagrangian; reversing it changes the multiplier sign. Apply the bordered, not unbordered, Hessian rule and place the gradient in the border. Check positivity and feasibility before giving an economic interpretation. Label the shadow-price calculation as an approximation for a finite change, and use an exact re-solve when an exact change is requested.
