PARTIAL DERIVATIVES · §§7.4–7.6

Which input changed?

Motivation

An equilibrium price is rarely controlled by one number. Demand and supply each have their own intercepts and slopes. When demand shifts, price and quantity respond; when supply shifts, their responses differ. The ordinary derivative of a function with one input does not say which parameter has moved. Chiang begins the transition to comparative statics by asking us to solve for an equilibrium outcome as a function of several independent parameters. A partial derivative then measures the outcome's response to one of them while the others are held at their current values.

The distinction between a market equation and a solved market matters. Differentiating demand alone describes movement along that demand schedule. Differentiating the equilibrium solution captures how demand and supply interact to reach a new price and quantity. The same idea appears in a national income model: a rise in government spending enters the income identity directly, but it also changes consumption through income. We will first learn to take partial slopes, then apply them to explicit equilibrium formulas. Finally, we will arrange partial derivatives in a Jacobian to study whether two outputs carry independent information.

Key ideas

$$f_x=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h,y)-f(x,y)}{h},\qquad f_y=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x,y+h)-f(x,y)}{h}$$

Picture a surface above the $x,y$ plane. Fixing $y$ cuts a curve through that surface; $f_x$ is its slope in the $x$ direction. Fixing $x$ gives the other cross section and $f_y$. This is the same derivative machinery as before, except that every other input acts like a constant. The pair $(f_x,f_y)$ is the gradient. Each component may still depend on both $x$ and $y$, because the slope along one cross section can change when we move to another cross section.

$$Q_d=a-bP,\quad Q_s=-c+dP,\qquad P^*=\frac{a+c}{b+d},\quad Q^*=\frac{ad-bc}{b+d}$$

For positive $b,d$, solve $Q_d=Q_s$ before calculating responses. Here $\partial P^*/\partial a=1/(b+d)>0$: a larger demand intercept lifts the equilibrium price. A larger $c$ lowers supply at every price, so $\partial Q^*/\partial c=-b/(b+d)<0$. The sign comes from the equations, not from the letter chosen for the parameter. Keeping an asterisk on equilibrium variables prevents confusion between a response of the whole market and a derivative of one schedule.

$$Y=C_0+\beta(Y-T)+I+G,\quad T=T_0+\delta Y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial Y^*}{\partial G}=\frac{1}{1-\beta(1-\delta)}$$

Substitute the tax equation into consumption, collect the terms in $Y$, and only then differentiate the reduced form. The multiplier exceeds the direct effect of one when $0<\beta<1$ and $0\le\delta<1$: induced consumption feeds back into income. An income-related tax weakens that feedback. These derivatives compare nearby equilibria under a specified model; they do not describe an arbitrary policy intervention outside it.

$$J=\begin{pmatrix}u_x&u_y\\v_x&v_y\end{pmatrix},\qquad |J|=u_xv_y-u_yv_x$$

The Jacobian collects the partial slopes of two functions in two inputs. If one output is constructed as a function of the other, their gradient rows are proportional and the determinant vanishes identically. A determinant equal to zero at one isolated point does not establish that relationship throughout the domain. Conversely, a nonzero determinant at a point shows the two rows there are independent. Our drills ask about an identically zero determinant rather than inferring a global relation from one selected value.

Worked examples

Two slopes on a production surface

Let $Q(K,L)=6\sqrt{KL}$ for positive inputs. Holding labor fixed, $MP_K=3\sqrt L/\sqrt K$; holding capital fixed, $MP_L=3\sqrt K/\sqrt L$. At $(K,L)=(4,9)$ these are $9/2$ and $2$, respectively. The different numbers reflect different cross sections.

A shifted market

With $Q_d=23-2P$ and $Q_s=-1+4P$, equality gives $P^*=4$ and $Q^*=15$. The reduced form is $P^*=(a+c)/(b+d)$, so $\partial P^*/\partial a=1/6$. The quantity response to a larger supply offset $c$ is $\partial Q^*/\partial c=-2/6=-1/3$.

Income feedback and a Jacobian

If $C=C_0+(1/2)(Y-T)$ and $T=T_0+Y/4$, the coefficient of $Y$ after substitution is $5/8$; the spending multiplier is $8/5$. Separately, if $u=2x+y$ and $v=3u^2$, then $(v_x,v_y)=6u(2,1)$, so the Jacobian determinant is identically zero.

For every response, name the parameter being varied and those being held fixed. This small habit makes a numerical derivative interpretable as a specific comparison between nearby model equilibria.

Common mistakes

Keep the nonchanging input fixed when taking a partial derivative. Solve the whole equilibrium before computing comparative-static responses. Check the sign of the specified supply parameter in its actual equation, and never claim global dependence from a single zero Jacobian value.
