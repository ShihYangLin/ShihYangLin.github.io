OPTIMAL TIMING · §§10.6–10.7

When should the asset be sold?

At a glance

  1. $A(t)=V(t)e^{-rt}$, $t\ge0$

    Choose the date that maximizes present value, not the future price. Selling now ($t=0$) is also an option. Why? ↓

  2. $\dfrac{V'(t^*)}{V(t^*)}=r$

    Keep waiting while the asset grows faster than the discount rate; sell when its growth rate falls to $r$. Why? ↓

  3. $\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\dfrac{V'}{V}\right)<0$ at $t^*$

    If the growth rate is falling as it crosses $r$, the candidate date is a maximum. Why? ↓

  4. $V=Ke^{k\sqrt t}\Rightarrow t^*=\left(\dfrac{k}{2r}\right)^2$, $V=Kb^{\sqrt t}\Rightarrow t^*=\left(\dfrac{\ln b}{2r}\right)^2$

    Solve for $\sqrt t$, then square it. The scale factor $K$ does not affect the date. Why? ↓

  5. Compare the interior date with $t=0$, and leave sunk purchase costs out of the timing derivative. Common mistakes ↓

Motivation

A wine dealer already owns a stock whose selling price rises as it ages. Selling today gives cash that can earn interest immediately; waiting may bring a higher future price. Comparing those nominal sale prices directly is misleading, because they arrive on different dates. The fix is to convert every possible future receipt to its value today. The question then becomes a one-variable optimization problem: choose the waiting time that maximizes present value. In the simple setup, the wine's original purchase cost is already paid and storage cost is set to zero, so neither changes the timing calculation.

The same problem appears with already planted timber. As trees grow, harvest value rises, but delaying harvest forgoes the return that sale proceeds could earn elsewhere. The value-growth curve can be written with a natural exponential, as in the wine example, or another base, as in the timber example. The base changes the derivative through a logarithm but not the decision principle. This module applies the exponential and log rules learned earlier to those two stories. Section 10.7 then names the key object explicitly: the instantaneous proportional growth rate of the asset's value.

Key ideas

$$A(t)=V(t)e^{-rt},\qquad t\ge0,\quad r>0$$

Present value $A(t)$ measures what a receipt at date $t$ is worth at date zero under a continuous discount rate $r$. The multiplication by $e^{-rt}$ puts all candidate sale dates on the same basis. The choice domain includes the boundary $t=0$: selling immediately is a legitimate option. If an interior optimum exists, we compare it with that boundary; otherwise the boundary may be best. A different cost of storage or planting decision would require a different objective, so the assumptions are stated in every problem.

↑ Back to summary

$$\frac{d\ln A}{dt}=\frac{V'(t)}{V(t)}-r,\qquad \frac{V'(t^*)}{V(t^*)}=r$$

Because value and present value are positive, taking logs preserves the date that maximizes the objective. The derivative separates into the asset's instantaneous proportional growth rate and the cost of waiting. While value grows faster than the discount rate, another short wait raises present value. Once it grows more slowly, waiting reduces present value. At an interior stationary date these two rates agree. This is a local first-order condition, so by itself it does not establish a maximum.

↑ Back to summary

$$\frac{d^2\ln A}{dt^2}=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{V'}{V}\right)<0\quad\text{at }t^*$$

If the asset's proportional value-growth rate decreases through $r$, the derivative of log present value switches from positive to negative. Then the candidate is a peak. For the square-root growth curves used here, this rate falls as $t$ increases: early growth is rapid, later gains arrive more slowly. The negative second derivative makes the local conclusion transparent, and the sign change over $t>0$ gives the global conclusion for this particular setup. We do not assume that every asset has declining growth or that a higher discount rate always shortens waiting under every possible value function.

↑ Back to summary

$$V(t)=K e^{k\sqrt t}\ \Longrightarrow\ t^*=\left(\frac{k}{2r}\right)^2,\qquad V(t)=K b^{\sqrt t}\ \Longrightarrow\ t^*=\left(\frac{\ln b}{2r}\right)^2$$

The two formulas express one calculation in different exponential bases. First solve the first-order equation for $\sqrt t$, then square to obtain the date in years. A common arithmetic slip is to report the value of $\sqrt t$ as the waiting time. The coefficient $K$ scales present value but cancels from its proportional growth rate, so it does not affect the best date in this zero-upkeep setup. At $t=0$ the derivative formula with $1/\sqrt t$ is unavailable, but the present-value function itself is defined, so the boundary still has to be considered.

Worked examples

Wine storage with a new growth curve

Suppose $V(t)=400e^{(4/5)\sqrt t}$ and $r=1/10$. Then $\ln A=\ln400+(4/5)\sqrt t-t/10$. Its derivative is $(2/5)/\sqrt t-1/10$, which vanishes at $\sqrt t=4$, so $t^*=16$. The second derivative is negative for positive $t$.

Timber and a nonnatural base

Suppose the harvest value is $V(t)=300\,3^{\sqrt t}$ with $r=(\ln3)/6$. Taking logs gives $d\ln A/dt=(\ln3)/(2\sqrt t)-r$. Setting this to zero gives $\sqrt t=3$, hence $t^*=9$. The growth rate declines with time, so this is a maximum.

The growth-rate interpretation

For $V(t)=200e^{\sqrt t}$, its value-growth rate is $d\ln V/dt=1/(2\sqrt t)$. At $t=4$ that rate is $1/4$. If the discount rate is $1/5$, the asset is still growing proportionally faster than the discount rate at that date, so a little more waiting raises present value.

Common mistakes

Optimize present value rather than the undiscounted future receipt. Do not include a sunk purchase or planting cost in a pure timing derivative. Square the solution for $\sqrt t$, check the second-order sign, and remember that $t=0$ belongs to the choice set.
