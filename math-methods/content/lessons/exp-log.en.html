Growth makes the exponent the variable

At a glance

  1. $B_m(t)=P\left(1+\dfrac{r}{m}\right)^{mt}$, $B_c(t)=Pe^{rt}$

    Continuous compounding is the limit as the compounding frequency $m$ grows; $e^{-rt}$ discounts a future amount to present value. Why? ↓

  2. $\ln(uv)=\ln u+\ln v$, $\ln(u/v)=\ln u-\ln v$, $\ln(u^a)=a\ln u$

    Logs turn products into sums and powers into coefficients. Every argument must be positive. Why? ↓

  3. $\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{u}=e^{u}u'$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}b^{u}=b^{u}(\ln b)u'$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln u=\dfrac{u'}{u}$

    Multiply by the inner derivative; a base other than $e$ adds a factor $\ln b$. Why? ↓

    Growth rates are log derivatives, so they add for products and subtract for quotients. Signed demand elasticity is negative. Why? ↓

Motivation

Earlier optimization examples used polynomials and rational functions, whose derivatives we already know. Economic growth and interest introduce a different shape: time itself appears in the exponent. A balance earning interest grows on its current balance; a growing asset may become more valuable the longer it is held. Before choosing when to sell or invest, we need to understand exponential functions, how to reverse them with logarithms, and how to differentiate both. Compounding gives the natural base $e$ a practical role: it is the limit of ever more frequent compounding, not a mysterious constant.

Suppose a fixed principal earns a nominal annual rate, credited several times each year. More frequent credits raise the balance because each earlier credit itself earns interest. As the number of credits grows without bound, the discrete formula approaches continuous compounding. A logarithm answers the inverse question: how long does a balance take to reach a specified target? It also simplifies products and quotients, making it useful for proportional growth and elasticity. This module builds the tools in that order. The next module uses them to choose an optimal sale or harvest date.

$$\ln(uv)=\ln u+\ln v,\quad \ln(u/v)=\ln u-\ln v,\quad \ln(u^a)=a\ln u\qquad(u,v>0)$$

The logarithm is the inverse of an exponential: $\ln(e^z)=z$, while $e^{\ln u}=u$ requires $u>0$. Its rules turn multiplication into addition and powers into coefficients, so an equation with an unknown exponent becomes a simpler equation after taking logs. In this site, both typed ln(x) and log(x) mean natural log; log(x,b) specifies a base. A log's argument must be positive in the real-number exercises.

↑ Back to summary

$$\frac{d}{dx}e^{u}=e^{u}u',\quad \frac{d}{dx}b^{u}=b^{u}(\ln b)u',\quad \frac{d}{dx}\ln u=\frac{u'}{u}$$

The natural exponential reproduces itself when differentiated. A different base adds $\ln b$, and a nested argument adds its own derivative through the chain rule. A logarithm changes at a rate relative to its argument: $u'/u$. This explains why taking logs before differentiating a long product can be easier than repeatedly using product and quotient rules. For a base-$b$ logarithm, divide again by $\ln b$.

$$g_y=\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt}=\frac{d\ln y}{dt},\qquad \varepsilon_{yx}=\frac{dy}{dx}\frac{x}{y}=\frac{d\ln y}{d\ln x}$$

An absolute derivative measures units of change per unit of input. A growth rate divides by the current positive level, making it a proportional change per unit of time. Thus a product's growth rates add and a quotient's subtract. Point elasticity compares proportional changes in two positive variables. Keep the sign: a downward-sloping demand curve has negative signed price elasticity; its absolute value is a separate quantity sometimes used in revenue formulas.

Base conversion links the two families of functions: $b^x=e^{x\ln b}$ and $\log_b x=\ln x/\ln b$. It is more than a notational trick. It lets one derivative rule handle every positive base, and it makes equivalent interest rates comparable. When solving an exponential equation, first isolate the exponential term; then take a logarithm of both positive sides. Taking a log of a negative or zero balance has no real-number meaning here.

Worked examples

From repeated credits to continuous growth

Place $300$ at a nominal annual rate $1/10$ for two years. With two credits per year the balance is $300(1+1/20)^4$; under continuous compounding it is $300e^{1/5}$. The second expression comes from a limiting compounding convention, not from merely changing the number of years.

A log equation and its derivative

Solve $5e^{3x}=5e^{12}$: divide by $5$, take natural logs, and obtain $3x=12$, so $x=4$. For a different task, $f(x)=\ln(3x+4)$ on $x>0$ has derivative $f'(x)=3/(3x+4)$; the numerator $3$ comes from the inner function.

Growth of a ratio and signed elasticity

If output is $Y=4e^{3t/10}$ and labor is $L=2e^{t/10}$, then $d\ln(Y/L)/dt=3/10-1/10=1/5$. If demand is $Q=28-2P$, then at $P=4$, $Q=20$ and $d\ln Q/d\ln P=(-2)(4/20)=-2/5$.

The logarithmic viewpoint is especially useful when a formula contains several multiplying components. It tracks each source of proportional change separately and keeps the economic interpretation visible during differentiation.

Common mistakes

Keep logarithm arguments positive. Do not omit the inner derivative or the $\ln b$ factor for a nonnatural base. Distinguish discrete from continuous compounding, and retain the negative sign of demand elasticity when a signed answer is requested.
