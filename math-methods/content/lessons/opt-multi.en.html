MULTIVARIABLE OPTIMIZATION · §§11.1–11.7

Choosing several quantities together

Motivation

A firm choosing two outputs cannot usually optimize each one in isolation. Producing more of one product may alter the cost of the other, and selling in different markets may change total production cost. The choice is a pair, not a point on a line. Imagine profit as a surface above the two-output plane: we seek a peak that can withstand a small change in either output or in both at once. This extends the single-choice problem, where a zero slope finds candidates and curvature helps identify their type.

At a smooth interior optimum, every first-order movement has zero effect on the objective. With two independent choices this means both partial derivatives vanish. Yet a surface can be flat at a saddle: it rises along one direction and falls along another. The second-order total differential summarizes curvature for a small movement in any direction. Its coefficients form the Hessian matrix. We start with two-variable surfaces and leading principal minors, then extend to several choice variables and economic applications. Our calculations stay with explicit objectives and their solutions; parameter changes are evaluated only when an explicit reduced form is available.

Key ideas

$$f_x(x^*,y^*)=f_y(x^*,y^*)=0,\qquad d^2f=f_{xx}(dx)^2+2f_{xy}dx\,dy+f_{yy}(dy)^2$$

The two first-order equations remove any linear gain from a tiny move. The remaining quadratic expression asks whether every nonzero direction lowers the objective, every direction raises it, or different directions disagree. For a maximum it must be negative in every nearby direction; for a minimum, positive. A cross-partial matters because a simultaneous change in both choices may behave differently from changing only one. Looking at $f_{xx}$ alone therefore cannot settle the two-choice question.

$$H=\begin{pmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\f_{xy}&f_{yy}\end{pmatrix},\quad D_1=f_{xx},\quad D_2=|H|=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$$

At a stationary point, $D_1<0,D_2>0$ makes the quadratic change negative in every direction and gives a strict local maximum. The pattern $D_1>0,D_2>0$ gives a strict local minimum. A negative determinant means opposite curvature in different directions, hence a saddle. If the determinant is zero, this test has no conclusion; higher-order terms can still decide the true shape. These are local tests. If the Hessian is negative definite throughout a convex feasible region, strict concavity promotes the stationary point to a unique global maximum there.

$$D_1<0,\ D_2>0,\ D_3<0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{negative definite in three variables}$$

For a three-choice Hessian, use the determinants of its upper-left blocks of size one, two, and three. The alternating pattern negative, positive, negative signals a strict local maximum; all three positive signal a strict local minimum. The full determinant by itself is insufficient, just as the two-variable determinant needs the first minor. If any required strict sign fails, these sufficient tests do not deliver the stated conclusion. This rule is a practical way to test the sign of the second-order differential without trying every direction separately.

$$\pi=P_1Q_1+P_2Q_2-C(Q_1,Q_2),\quad P_i=C_{Q_i};\qquad MR_1=MR_2=MC(Q_1+Q_2)$$

For a competitive multiproduct firm, each output price equals its marginal cost at an interior optimum. A cost interaction means each marginal cost depends on both outputs, so the equations must be solved together. For separate markets served by one producer, each market's marginal revenue equals the same marginal cost of total production. Different inverse-demand curves generally give different prices even though the marginal revenues agree. After solving, confirm quantities and prices are positive where the economic setup requires them, then test the profit Hessian. The first-order equalities identify candidates; the curvature and feasible domain justify an optimum.

Worked examples

Peak versus saddle

Let $f(x,y)=10-(x-2)^2-2(y-4)^2$. Its partial derivatives vanish at $(2,4)$. Here $H=\begin{pmatrix}-2&0\\0&-4\end{pmatrix}$, so $D_1=-2$ and $D_2=8$: a strict maximum. By comparison, $h(x,y)=(x-2)^2-(y-4)^2$ has Hessian determinant $-4$, so its stationary point is a saddle.

Two products with shared costs

Suppose $C=2Q_1^2+Q_1Q_2+Q_2^2+3Q_1+2Q_2+5$ and prices are $P_1=16$, $P_2=7$. The first-order equations are $16=4Q_1+Q_2+3$ and $7=Q_1+2Q_2+2$, giving $(Q_1,Q_2)=(3,1)$. The profit Hessian is $\begin{pmatrix}-4&-1\\-1&-2\end{pmatrix}$; minors $-4,7$ prove strict concavity and a unique global maximum over nonnegative outputs.

Sales in separate markets

Let $P_1=27-Q_1$, $P_2=25-2Q_2$, and $C=3(Q_1+Q_2)+(Q_1+Q_2)^2$. Then $MR_1=27-2Q_1$, $MR_2=25-4Q_2$, and $MC=3+2(Q_1+Q_2)$. Solving the two equalities $MR_i=MC$ gives $(Q_1,Q_2)=(5,2)$ and $MC=17$. Prices are $22$ and $21$, both positive; the profit Hessian has minors $-4,20$, giving a strict maximum.

Common mistakes

Do not stop when both first partials are zero. Check all nearby directions through the Hessian, and do not call a zero determinant a saddle. Keep the order and signs of leading principal minors straight. In economic problems, include cost interactions and common marginal cost in both first-order equations, and check whether the proposed choices lie in the meaningful positive region.
