DERIVATIVE BASICS · §§6.2–6.3, 7.1

From a finite change to a marginal effect

At a glance

  1. $f'(x)=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$

    The derivative is the limit of secant slopes, that is, the tangent slope at $x$. Cancel $h$ before letting $h\to0$. Why? ↓

  2. $\dfrac{d}{dx}c=0$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}(ax^n)=anx^{n-1}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}(u+v)=u'+v'$

    Differentiate term by term. On a valid domain, the power rule also covers negative and fractional powers. Why? ↓

  3. $MC(Q)=\dfrac{dC}{dQ}$, $y=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0)$

    Marginal cost is the slope of total cost. A tangent line needs both the slope and the point on the curve. Why? ↓

  4. Differentiate first, then substitute: $f'(x)$ is a function, while $f'(x_0)$ is one number. Common mistakes ↓

Motivation

Comparative statics asks how an outcome responds when a parameter changes. For a small but finite change, we can measure the average response. Shrinking that change gives an instantaneous rate, such as the marginal cost of producing a little more output.

Comparative statics asks how an endogenous outcome changes when an exogenous quantity changes. Even in a simple one-parameter model, we must distinguish a total change from a change per unit of the parameter. Start at $x$, move by a finite increment $h$, and compare $f(x+h)$ with $f(x)$. Dividing their difference by $h$ gives an average response over that move. This quantity still depends on the move's size. If the response varies along the curve, two different increments from the same starting point can produce different average rates.

Economists often want a marginal effect at one specified value rather than an average over a long interval. By bringing $h$ closer and closer to zero, we ask what rate the finite quotient approaches. We never evaluate the original quotient at $h=0$, where it would divide by zero; we evaluate its limit after simplification. The geometric counterpart is a secant line becoming a tangent line. For a total-cost curve, the tangent slope is marginal cost when output can be treated as continuous. This sequence gives the derivative both a precise calculation and a concrete economic meaning before introducing shortcuts.

Key ideas

$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\quad(h\ne0),\qquad f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

The quotient is the slope of a secant line joining two points. As $h$ approaches zero, its limit is the tangent slope. The quotient depends on both $x$ and $h$; the derivative function depends on $x$ alone.

$$\frac{d}{dx}c=0,\qquad \frac{d}{dx}(ax^n)=anx^{n-1},\qquad \frac{d}{dx}(u+v)=u'+v'$$

A constant has no response to $x$. The power rule gives a quick way to find the slope without rebuilding a limit each time, including for negative and fractional powers where the domain permits them. Differentiate a function first, then evaluate it at a point.

↑ Back to summary

$$MC(Q)=\frac{dC}{dQ},\qquad y-f(x_0)=f'(x_0)(x-x_0)$$

Marginal cost is the slope of the total-cost curve when output is treated as continuous. The same derivative supplies the slope for a tangent line through $(x_0,f(x_0))$.

↑ Back to summary

A derivative function gives the marginal rate at each permissible input. Substituting one value afterward gives a derivative value at that point. The distinction matters when a firm compares marginal costs at different output levels: a single number does not describe the whole cost schedule. A tangent line also needs both pieces of information, the slope and the point on the original curve. Its local linear expression predicts nearby values; it is not generally the same as the curved cost function away from that point.

Chapter 7 replaces repeated limit expansions with differentiation rules. A fixed additive cost disappears from marginal cost because its derivative is zero, while a coefficient multiplying a changing term remains. The sum rule works term by term for a polynomial, and the power rule applies to negative or fractional powers on their valid domains. Keep those domains explicit: $x^{-2}$ excludes zero, and the real square root example is taken on $x>0$. These shortcuts inherit the limit definition; they do not change what the derivative measures. The later product, quotient, and chain rules are needed when economic quantities combine several changing pieces.

Worked examples

Difference quotient before the limit

Let $f(x)=4x^2+3x+7$. Expanding $f(x+h)$ and subtracting $f(x)$ leaves $8xh+4h^2+3h$. Divide by $h\ne0$ to get $8x+4h+3$. As $h\to0$, the derivative is $f'(x)=8x+3$.

Tangent and marginal cost

Suppose $C(Q)=18+5Q+2Q^2$. Then $MC(Q)=5+4Q$, so at $Q=3$ the tangent slope is $17$. Since $C(3)=51$, its tangent is $C=51+17(Q-3)$.

A fractional power

For $x>0$, let $f(x)=6\sqrt{x}+4x$. Rewrite the root as $6x^{1/2}$, apply the power rule, and obtain $f'(x)=3x^{-1/2}+4=3/\sqrt{x}+4$.

Common mistakes

Do not set $h=0$ before cancelling it. Do not confuse the derivative function with its value at one point. Keep the fixed-cost derivative at zero, and respect $x>0$ for fractional or negative-power examples here.
