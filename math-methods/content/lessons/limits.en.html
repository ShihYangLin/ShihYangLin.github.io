LIMITS & CONTINUITY · §§6.2–6.7

What happens near a point?

At a glance

  1. $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ $\iff\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=L$

    A two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits agree. A value that grows without bound diverges to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$; it is not a finite limit. Why? ↓

  2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}=\frac{\lim_{x\to a}u(x)}{\lim_{x\to a}v(x)}$ if $\lim_{x\to a}v(x)\ne0$

    Polynomials: substitute directly. Quotients: substitute only when the denominator's limit is nonzero; for $0/0$, simplify first. At infinity, compare the highest powers. Why? ↓

  3. $f$ continuous at $a$ $\iff\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$

    The value must be defined, the limit must exist, and the two must match. Continuity does not imply differentiability: a corner is continuous but has no single slope. Why? ↓

  4. $0/0$ is not an answer: never report it as zero, and check both sides of the point. Common mistakes ↓

Motivation

An instantaneous response in comparative statics comes from shrinking a finite change toward zero. Before taking that step, we need to ask what a function approaches near a point, separately from its value at the point.

Comparative statics begins by comparing an old equilibrium with a new one after an exogenous condition changes. To describe the response per unit of change, divide the change in the outcome by the change in the parameter. That difference quotient is an average response over an interval. If we want the response at a particular starting point, we let the interval shrink. That is why limits come before continuity and derivatives: the derivative is itself a limit. A limit asks what nearby values approach, even if the function is not defined at the exact destination.

There are two separate questions whenever a graph approaches a point. From which side are we approaching, and what value has the function been assigned at the point? The first question governs whether a two-sided limit exists; the second matters only when we ask about continuity. Imagine a tax schedule that changes abruptly at a threshold. Approaching the threshold from below may give a different outcome from approaching above it. By contrast, a removable discontinuity can have the same approach from either side while still lacking an assigned value. These examples make the definitions useful for economic functions that have thresholds, exclusions, or piecewise rules.

Key ideas

$$\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=L_-,\qquad \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=L_+$$

The two-sided finite limit exists only if $L_-=L_+$. A jump has different approaches from the two sides. If the function grows without bound, describe divergence to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$; infinity is not a finite number.

$$\lim_{x\to a}\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}=\frac{\lim_{x\to a}u(x)}{\lim_{x\to a}v(x)}\quad\text{if }\lim_{x\to a}v(x)\ne0$$

Limits of polynomials can be found by substitution. Quotient substitution works only when the denominator's limit is nonzero. At a removable discontinuity, simplify for nearby $x$ first; cancellation does not assign a value at the missing point. At infinity, compare the highest powers.

$$f\text{ continuous at }a\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(a)\text{ is defined},\ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)\text{ exists},\ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$$

Continuity adds the function's assigned value to the limit question. A corner can be continuous while its left and right slopes differ, so continuity alone does not guarantee differentiability.

When a denominator tends to zero, ordinary quotient substitution is not licensed. Sometimes a common factor can be cancelled for every nearby input except the excluded point, leaving a simpler function whose approach is easy to evaluate. That cancellation establishes the limit but does not redefine the original function. At infinity the question changes: we compare how fast the dominant powers grow as the input increases without bound. A ratio may tend to a finite number, or it may diverge with a positive or negative sign. Treating infinity as a real answer would erase this distinction.

The domain is part of every limit statement. Inequalities and absolute values describe which inputs belong to a function's domain; the same description tells us whether an approach from a side is possible. Limit laws then save us from constructing a table of nearby values every time. They justify direct evaluation of a polynomial and a rational function with a nonzero denominator limit. Finally, continuity joins the nearby behavior to the point's actual value. A continuous curve can still have a sharp corner: matching values from left and right does not force the left and right tangent slopes to agree.

Worked examples

A removable discontinuity

For $x\ne3$, let $f(x)=(x-3)(2x+5)/(x-3)$. Cancel only for nearby $x$ to get $2x+5$. Hence $\lim_{x\to3}f(x)=11$, even though $f(3)$ is undefined.

A step at a threshold

Suppose $f(x)=2x+1$ for $x<2$ and $f(x)=3x-1$ for $x\ge2$. The left approach is $5$ and the right approach is also $5$, so the limit is $5$. If the second branch instead were $3x+1$, the right approach would be $7$ and the two-sided limit would not exist.

Continuity and behavior at infinity

For $f(x)=x+4$ except $f(2)=9$, the limit at $2$ is $6$ but the value is $9$. Assigning $f(2)=6$ repairs continuity. For $g(x)=(3x+2)/(2x^2+1)$, divide by $x^2$ to see $g(x)\to0$ as $x\to+\infty$.

Common mistakes

Do not substitute into $0/0$ and call it zero. Do not use the quotient limit law with a zero denominator limit. Check both sides and distinguish the approach value from the value actually assigned at the point.
