ONE-VARIABLE OPTIMIZATION · §§9.1–9.4, 9.6

Where does a choice become best?

At a glance

  1. $f'(x^*)=0$, and $f'$ changes sign at $x^*$ from positive to negative $\Rightarrow$ local max; from negative to positive $\Rightarrow$ local min

    Find the stationary points, then check whether the slope changes sign across each one. Why? ↓

  2. $f'(x^*)=0$, $f''(x^*)<0\Rightarrow$ local max, $f''(x^*)>0\Rightarrow$ local min

    The quick test is the sign of the second derivative at a stationary point. If $f''(x^*)=0$, this test is silent. Why? ↓

  3. $MR(Q^*)=MC(Q^*)$, $\pi''(Q^*)<0$

    Profit peaks where marginal revenue equals marginal cost and profit bends downward. Keep $Q>0$ and $P(Q)>0$. Why? ↓

  4. $f'(x^*)=\cdots=f^{(n-1)}(x^*)=0$, $f^{(n)}(x^*)\ne0$

    When $f''(x^*)=0$, use the first nonzero derivative. For even $n$, a negative value gives a max and a positive value a min. For odd $n$, there is no extremum. Why? ↓

  5. $f'=0$ only gives candidates. A global answer must also compare endpoints and cover the whole feasible set. Why? ↓ · Common mistakes ↓

Motivation

Economics studies choices among feasible alternatives. A firm chooses output, a household chooses consumption, and each choice is judged by a stated goal. A high value of a mathematical function is useful only after we identify what the function measures, what can be chosen, and which choices are allowed. For a firm, profit is revenue less cost, $\pi(Q)=R(Q)-C(Q)$; output $Q$ is the choice variable. The most profitable output need not be the largest possible output. It is the output at which the objective is greatest within the feasible set.

A graph can suggest a peak or valley, but calculus locates it precisely. The first derivative tells whether a small increase in the choice variable raises or lowers the objective. At a smooth interior optimum the slope must be zero, yet a flat point can also be an inflection. We therefore move from finding stationary points to testing signs around them, then use curvature and higher derivatives as quicker tests. Throughout, a local conclusion concerns nearby choices. To claim a best choice over the entire economic domain, we must also consider the domain, boundaries, and the objective's overall shape.

Key ideas

$$f'(x^*)=0,\quad \begin{cases} f'(x)>0, & x^*-\delta<x<x^*\\ f'(x)<0, & x^*<x<x^*+\delta \end{cases}\;\Longrightarrow\;f(x^*)\ \text{is a local maximum}$$

Imagine walking along the graph from left to right. Positive slope means the objective rises as the choice grows; negative slope means it falls. A change from rising to falling identifies a nearby peak. A change from falling to rising identifies a nearby trough. If the slope keeps the same sign on both sides of a stationary point, the curve passes through a flat spot without turning. The derivative must exist for the smooth interior rule; a boundary or a sharp point requires separate attention. Here $\delta>0$ is some small distance; reversing both inequalities gives the condition for a local minimum.

↑ Back to summary

$$f''(x)=\frac{d}{dx}f'(x),\qquad f'(x^*)=0,\quad f''(x^*)<0\Rightarrow\text{local maximum},\quad f''(x^*)>0\Rightarrow\text{local minimum}$$

The second derivative records how the slope changes. Negative curvature means successive slopes become smaller; at a zero slope the curve bends downward, so nearby values lie below the stationary value. Positive curvature bends upward and yields a local minimum. A zero second derivative does not decide the classification. It can occur at a flat minimum, a flat maximum, or an inflection. An inflection requires an actual change in concavity across the point; solving $f''(x)=0$ merely supplies a candidate.

↑ Back to summary

$$\pi'(Q)=MR(Q)-MC(Q)=0,\qquad \pi''(Q)=R''(Q)-C''(Q)$$

For an interior profit choice, marginal revenue equals marginal cost. This equality balances the extra receipt from another unit against its extra cost, but does not by itself establish a maximum. A negative second derivative gives a local profit maximum. If profit is strictly concave throughout a feasible interval and the stationary output is inside it, that output is also its unique global maximum on the interval. For demand $P(Q)=a-bQ$ with $a,b>0$, keep $Q>0$ and $P(Q)>0$ when interpreting the result; an algebraic root outside that region is not an admissible positive-price output.

↑ Back to summary

$$f'(x^*)=f''(x^*)=\cdots=f^{(n-1)}(x^*)=0,\quad f^{(n)}(x^*)\ne0$$

When the second derivative is zero, continue until the first nonzero derivative. If its order is even, its sign decides the nearby extremum: positive gives a local minimum and negative a local maximum. If its order is odd, the leading change switches sign across the center, giving neither maximum nor minimum; in the smooth power examples here it is a stationary inflection. This is why a fourth-power valley survives a zero second derivative, while a cubic flat spot does not become an optimum.

↑ Back to summary

Keep the objective value distinct from the choice variable. A peak in $\pi(Q)$ identifies a particular $Q^*$; the statement “profit is maximized” refers to $\pi(Q^*)$, not to output being mathematically maximal. When several stationary points occur, classify every one rather than choosing the first root. On a closed interval, compare all local candidates with endpoints if a global answer is requested. On an unbounded domain, examine whether the objective actually attains a largest or smallest value. These checks connect the calculus test back to the economic decision.

Worked examples

Two stationary choices

Let $f(x)=2x^3-3x^2-12x+5$. Then $f'(x)=6x^2-6x-12=6(x+1)(x-2)$, so the stationary choices are $-1$ and $2$. Around $-1$, the slope changes from positive to negative, giving a local maximum. Around $2$, it changes from negative to positive, giving a local minimum. The two roots cannot be assigned the same classification.

Profit with a feasible output

Suppose $P(Q)=31-2Q$ and $C(Q)=3Q^2+5Q+2$. Then $MR=31-4Q$, $MC=6Q+5$, and $MR=MC$ at $Q^*=13/5$. Price there is $129/5>0$. Also $\pi''=-10<0$ at every feasible output, so this is a local maximum and, by strict concavity, the unique global maximum on the positive-price interval.

A flat point and curvature

For $g(x)=(x-3)^4+2$, both $g'(3)$ and $g''(3)$ vanish, but $g^{(4)}(3)=24>0$: $x=3$ is a local minimum. For $h(x)=2(x+1)^3+5$, $h''(x)=12(x+1)$ changes from negative to positive at $x=-1$. That point is an inflection, even though $h'(-1)=0$.

Common mistakes

Do not call every solution of $f'=0$ a maximum. Check a sign change or a sufficient curvature condition. Do not treat $f''=0$ as proof of inflection; inspect curvature on both sides. In an economic problem, reject negative output and negative prices where the model excludes them, and reserve global language for an argument covering the full feasible set.
